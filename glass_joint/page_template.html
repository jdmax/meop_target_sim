What loads the glass

From 293 K to 4 K, aluminum contracts 0.415 % and Stycast 2850FT 0.51 %, but Pyrex only 0.056 %. Inside the bond, the metal and epoxy squeeze the glass radially by about 14 µm. That puts the bonded glass in compression, which glass tolerates easily. Where the bond ends, the wall has to bend from its squeezed radius back to its free radius over a shell length of about √(Rt) ≈ 1.5 mm. That bending puts the outer surface into axial tension just outside the bond edge, and that is where a ring crack would start.

The two joints differ in how much of the 0.36 % mismatch they can force onto the glass. A thick 6061-T6 collar stays elastic and imposes nearly all of it. A thin annealed 1100 feather yields at about 40 MPa, so its hoop force is capped and it drags the glass much less. The axial thermal gradient along the free span plays no real part. A thin tube follows a smooth strain variation almost stress-free.

The load builds while the joint is still warm. By 150 K, the surface stress is at 69 % (collar) and 76 % (feather) of its 2 K value; by 77 K it is at 91–92 %. Below about 50 K almost nothing changes, because none of the three materials contracts appreciably there.

For the joint you are building

  • Keep the feather dead soft. Use 1100-O and anneal it after machining. A feather that yields at 100 MPa instead of 40 raises the edge stress by about 70 %, and an elastic one matches the solid collar. Any cold work from turning the taper is lost margin.
  • Make the tip thin and the feather wall thin. A 0.03 mm tip cuts the edge stress by 16 % compared with 0.1 mm. A 0.5 mm wall cuts it by 18 %, and a 9 mm taper by 11 %. A blunt 0.5 mm tip raises it by 60 %.
  • Keep the bond line thin and the epoxy compliant. The epoxy itself contracts more than the aluminum. A 40 µm bond line lowers the stress by 15 %, while 150 µm raises it by 28 %.
  • Finish the bond edge carefully. The model's sharp epoxy corner is the one thing it cannot resolve, and it is the most loaded spot. Leave a small, smooth fillet tapering onto the glass with no voids. Keep any roughening of the glass under the bond, stopping about 1 mm short of the edge. The 2 mm of bare glass next to the edge is where the tension sits.
  • Your LN₂ dunks are close to a full proof test. At 77 K the joint already carries 91–92 % of its 2 K surface stress. A joint that survives repeated dunks and a leak check has seen nearly its full service load at the glass.
  • Check the deep end of the bond. Where the feather joins the thick cell body at the glass tip, the elastic bond shear reaches 60–70 MPa in this geometry. The glass there is in compression and safe, but the epoxy may craze. That is a leak-path question for the He leak check, not a glass-fracture one.

Method and checks

Solver, verification, and limits

Axisymmetric small-strain thermoelasticity on 9-node Lagrange quadrilaterals with 3 × 3 Gauss integration. The mesh is graded to 5 µm at the bond edge, about 26,000 nodes. Thermal strain is the integrated contraction from 293 K at each Gauss point. Aluminum uses J2 plasticity with radial return and a consistent tangent, and each 30-minute snapshot is one Newton load step. The only constraint is axial, at the far corner of the aluminum block; the warm end of the glass is free.

Verification. An all-glass model under uniform contraction reproduces the analytic radial displacement to 0.05 %. The fracture weight function reproduces K = 1.12σ√(πa) for uniform stress to 0.4 %. Mesh convergence is in the table below.

Limits.

  • The Pyrex and Stycast contraction curves are single-Einstein shapes fitted to tabulated totals. The 2 K result depends only on those totals; the intermediate snapshots carry the shape uncertainty.
  • The epoxy is elastic, which overstates bond-edge stresses where real Stycast would yield or craze.
  • The long ring crack is conservative compared with a semi-elliptical thumbnail flaw of the same depth.
  • Radiation onto the free span would warm it and move the gradient, but it barely affects the joint, which sits at the sink temperature.
  • KIC is taken at its room-temperature value; Pyrex toughness changes little on cooling. With moisture absent below about 200 K, stress-corrosion crack growth does not apply.
